AQUAFLOW - MODELS AND DATA: EVERYTHING FOR THE VIVA
====================================================

Read this top to bottom once. Section 12 has likely questions with short answers, and section 13 is a
one-page cheat sheet of the numbers.


1. THE PROJECT IN FIVE SENTENCES
--------------------------------
AquaFlow is a smart water-meter system. An ESP32 with a YF-S201 flow sensor measures how much water a
household uses and posts a reading about every 6 seconds to a Node.js backend (MongoDB). The web app
shows live flow, usage history, bills and prepaid balance. On top of that I added two machine-learning
models: (1) a LEAK / BURST DETECTOR that raises alerts, and (2) a USAGE FORECAST that predicts the next
7 days of water use and turns that into a prepaid "run-out date", a postpaid "bill estimate" and a
daily-goal tracker. Both run in a small Python service (FastAPI) that the backend calls.

The physical test rig is a "loop": a small pump circulating water through the sensor. Its flow is about
3.5 L/min and never above about 4 L/min. A real house has mains pressure and much bigger flows, so
everything here is "loop scale".


2. HOW THE PIECES FIT TOGETHER
------------------------------
  ESP32 + flow sensor  --POST /api/readings (flowRate, volume)-->  Backend (Node.js, MongoDB)
                                                                       |
         every 60 s, for each online device: last 12 h of readings     |   GET /api/forecast, /api/goals
                                                                       v
                                                      Python model service (FastAPI, port 8001)
                                                        /score    -> leak model (Random Forest)
                                                        /forecast -> forecast model (gradient boosting)
                                                                       |
  Alerts page, Usage page (forecast + goal), admin Usage Forecast page <-- backend --

Folders (all under edge-ai/):
  data/loop_household   generator for ONE household calibrated on the loop (used as an extra test set)
  data/simdeum          generator for 30 varied households (main training data)
  data/real             export of REAL readings from MongoDB (not used for training)
  leak_detection        leak model: code, training script, results, Colab notebook
  usage_forecast        forecast model: code, training script, results, weather/holiday code
  service               the FastAPI service the backend calls

The backend (in backend/) also contains an older RULE-BASED detector. It is kept as the baseline and as
the automatic valve shut-off for very large flows.


3. THE DATA - WHAT IS REAL AND WHAT IS SIMULATED
-------------------------------------------------
This is the most important thing to be honest and clear about in a viva.

3.1 What is real
  - Real readings from the physical device, stored in MongoDB. Used ONLY to measure how the loop behaves
    (flow level, noise, timing) so the simulator can be calibrated. They are NOT used to train or test
    the models, because they are too sparse: the device only sent data while the loop was running, which
    covered about 1% of the time between the first and last reading.
  - Real weather (Open-Meteo) and real Sri Lankan public holidays, used as inputs to the forecast.

3.2 What the loop measurements say (used to calibrate the simulator)
  - Sensor: YF-S201, 450 pulses per litre. The firmware turns pulses in the last interval into litres
    and L/min.
  - Pump flow: mean about 3.49 L/min. Each run settles at its own level (3.27 to 3.78), drifts slowly
    during the run, and has reading noise of about 0.07 L/min. Occasional brief dips.
  - Reading spacing: median 5.77 s (5th to 95th percentile 5.60 to 6.60 s).
  - Missed readings: about 1.9% of gaps are longer than normal (9 s up to 2 minutes).
  - Idle noise: about 4.9% of idle readings are non-zero (about half of those are a single pulse = 0.02 L/min,
    the rest 0.05 to 0.30 L/min).
  - Water used during a missed reading is lost, not added to the next one (firmware behaviour).

3.3 Simulated data used for the LEAK model (data/simdeum/out)
  - Generated with pySIMDEUM (Blokker, Vreeburg and van Dijk, 2010, "Simulating residential water demand
    with a stochastic end-use model"). It builds a random household (people, jobs, fixtures) and simulates
    every fixture's per-second use: toilets, taps, showers, washing machine, etc.
  - 30 households: 10 one-person, 10 two-person, 10 families. 25 follow a daytime routine and 5 are
    night-shift households (routine shifted 10 hours).
  - 21 days each (1 to 21 October 2026).
  - Loop adaptation: SIMDEUM assumes mains pressure (a shower is about 8.5 L/min). All flows are scaled
    by ONE constant so a shower matches the loop's pump level, each run gets its own pump level, and the
    total is capped at the pump maximum (about 3.95 L/min). Garden watering and tank filling (5 to 30
    minutes near pump level) are added as hard normal cases so "long flow" alone does not mean "burst".
  - Then the loop's sensor model (section 3.2) turns the per-second flow into readings with real
    spacing, missed readings, pulse rounding, noise and dips.
  - Injected anomalies per household: 4 micro-leaks and 2 bursts = 180 in total (120 micro-leaks and 60
    bursts). Shapes:
        steady trickle     0.15 to 1.5 L/min, hours long, under normal use
        leaking cistern   pump-level refills every 1.5 to 5 minutes
        growing leak       starts tiny and grows
        burst              the pump flat out, non-stop, 20 to 75 minutes
    All anomalies have exact start and end times, so the answer key is exact.
  - A burst on this loop is capped at pump level (about 3.5 L/min). It is NOT above the backend's old
    30 L/min rule, so that rule can never see it. The model has to recognise a burst by DURATION and
    CONTINUITY, not by size.

3.4 Extra test sets for the leak model
  - data/loop_household/out: one 4-person household, 1 to 21 Oct, 3 micro-leaks and 2 bursts. Made by a
    different generator (not SIMDEUM), so it is a partial check that the model is not just memorising the
    SIMDEUM generator.
  - data/loop_household/out_seeds: 8 more datasets of 21 days each with 5 anomalies each (seeds never used
    in training): 24 micro-leaks and 16 bursts in total.

3.5 Simulated data for the FORECAST model
  - The same 30 SIMDEUM households, but 10 weeks (1 July to 9 September 2026) of NORMAL use only (no leaks).
    Daily litres are summed per Sri Lanka calendar day.
  - Finding: this data has NO weekly pattern and NO day-to-day memory (daily variation about 36% of the
    average, weekend/weekday ratio 1.02, correlation with yesterday about 0). The simulator redraws each
    day independently. So the best possible forecast is the household's own average.
  - Because real households do have patterns, I made a second dataset (usage_forecast/data/
    patterned_daily.csv) by multiplying each day by ASSUMED factors (set out in make_patterned_daily.py):
        weekend uplift       +0 to 25% per household
        absence spells       about one per 4 weeks, 2 to 6 days, usage x 0.1
        guest spells         about one per 3 weeks, 1 to 3 days, usage x 1.3
        slow drift           -0.1% to +0.1% per day
  - A third dataset (patterned_weather_daily.csv) also applies ASSUMED reactions to the REAL weather and
    holidays of those dates: +3% per degree of daily maximum above 29 C, -8% on rainy days (5 mm or more),
    +10% on public holidays.
  - THESE EFFECT SIZES ARE MY ASSUMPTIONS, NOT MEASUREMENTS. A model improving on them shows the pipeline
    works, not that real households behave that way.

3.6 Sample data written into the database for the demo
  - Devices ESP32-A1 and ESP32-A2 contain generated "sample history" readings (tagged synthetic:true in
    the database) so the forecast and goal screens have something to show. They come from the loop
    household generator. They are not measurements and must be described as such.


4. THE LEAK / BURST MODEL
-------------------------
4.1 The task
  Every minute, say whether the household's recent flow looks like: normal use, a micro-leak, or a burst.
  The model outputs a probability for each of the three classes. An alarm is raised when a class
  probability stays high long enough (see 4.4).

4.2 Input: features (computed from past data only - no looking into the future)
  Readings are first averaged into 1-minute values. A minute is "idle" if its mean flow is below 0.1 L/min
  and "high" if it is 2.5 L/min or more (pump level). From the last minutes the model builds 27 features
  in three groups:
    Continuity (16)  how long flow has run without stopping; how long since the house was last quiet
                     (10 or 30 min); the fraction of the last 10/30/60/180 minutes that were idle; the
                     minimum flow over the last 10/30/60/180 minutes; the fraction of high-flow minutes;
                     the number of on/off switches in the last hour.
    Level (9)        current flow; mean and standard deviation over 10/30/60 minutes; the maximum over
                     10 minutes; litres in the last hour.
    Time of day (2)  sine and cosine of the local hour.
  A fourth group, "house baseline" (comparison with the house's own last week), was tested and then
  REMOVED from the final model because it did not help (see the ablation in 4.6).
  Why these: a leak is about flow that never stops. A normal tap use or pump run lasts seconds to a couple
  of minutes. A leak keeps minimum flow above zero for hours; a burst keeps the pump at full level for far
  longer than any fixture.

4.3 The algorithm: Random Forest
  A Random Forest classifier: 150 decision trees, at least 20 samples per leaf, balanced class weights
  (so the rare leak minutes are not drowned out by normal minutes). Training set: every anomaly minute
  plus a random 25% of normal minutes from the training households.
  Why a Random Forest: it works well on tabular, engineered features; it needs little tuning; it gives
  probabilities; it is fast enough to run for every device every minute; and it can be inspected
  (feature importance). I also trained gradient boosting and an Isolation Forest to compare.
    - Gradient boosting: similar recall but more false alarms (0.14 per day against 0.08).
    - Isolation Forest: UNSUPERVISED. It is trained only on normal minutes and flags anything unusual, so
      it needs no leak labels. It was worse (burst recall 75%, micro-leak delay 59 minutes) but it is the
      option if labelled leaks are not available in a real deployment.

4.4 Turning probabilities into alarms
  For each class (micro_leak, burst) an alarm is active when its probability is at or above a THRESHOLD
  for at least PERSIST consecutive minutes. The two numbers per class were chosen automatically on
  validation households: of all combinations (threshold 0.3 to 0.98, persistence 1 to 30 minutes), keep
  those that stay under the false-alarm budget (0.05 per day per alarm) and pick the one with the highest
  recall, then the shortest delay. Final values:
        micro-leak alarm:  probability >= 0.8 for 2 minutes
        burst alarm:       probability >= 0.5 for 20 minutes
  The burst alarm needs 20 minutes, which is why bursts are detected after about 19 minutes on average
  (they are 20 to 75 minutes long). That long persistence is what separates a burst from a long garden
  watering session.

4.5 How it was trained and tested
  - 6-fold cross-validation BY HOUSEHOLD: the 30 households are split into 6 groups of 5. Each group is
    tested by a model trained on households it has never seen. This matters because a model can memorise
    one household's routine; testing on new households shows whether it generalises.
  - Inside each fold, some households are used to tune the alarm thresholds, never the test ones.
  - The final saved model is trained on households 1 to 25 and its alarm settings tuned on 26 to 30.
  - Metrics are EVENT-level, not minute accuracy:
        caught         the alarm was active at some minute between the event's start and end
        delay          minutes from the event's start to the first alarm minute
        false alarm    an alarm that overlaps no anomaly (with a 30-minute grace after each one)
        false alarms per day   counted over the whole test period
    Plain accuracy would be meaningless: about 99% of minutes are normal, so a model that never
    alarms would score 99%.

4.6 Results (cross-validation on the 30 simulated households, Random Forest)
        Micro-leaks caught   99%  (119 of 120)    median delay 10 minutes
        Bursts caught        92%  (55 of 60)      median delay 19 minutes
        False alarms         0.08 +/- 0.05 per day (about one every two weeks per house)
  Other detectors, same test:
        Gradient boosting    micro 100%, burst 90%, 0.14 false alarms per day
        Isolation Forest     micro 92%,  burst 75%, 0.16 per day, delays 59 and 33 minutes
        Old backend rules    0% and 0%  (see the question in section 12)
  By anomaly shape: steady 42/42, growing 39/39 (found in 6 minutes), leaking cistern 38/39 (26 min),
  burst 55/60.
  Ablation (what happens when features are removed): without time of day - about the same; without time
  and flow level - recall the same but micro-leak detection slows to 30 minutes; without the house
  baseline - slightly better, so it was dropped.
  False alarms by household type: family 0.10, one-person 0.076, two-person 0.062 per day; by routine:
  day 0.07, night-shift 0.11 per day.
  Extra test sets: the single-household set 3/3 micro-leaks and 2/2 bursts; the 8 unseen 21-day datasets
  24/24 micro-leaks and 16/16 bursts with 0.01 false alarms per day.
  The standard deviations (+/-) are across the 6 folds.

4.7 How it behaves when the system is running
  - Every 60 seconds the backend takes each ONLINE device, collects its last 12 hours of readings and
    sends them to the service. The service averages them into minutes, drops the minute still in progress,
    clamps flows above 6 L/min (loop scale), builds the features and runs the model.
  - Needs at least 30 minutes of history, and ignores a device silent for more than 3 minutes.
  - An alarm counts as active if it fired at any time in the last 10 minutes (a leaking cistern has quiet
    minutes between refills).
  - The backend creates an alert marked source "model" (warning for micro-leak, critical for burst) and
    shows it in the Alerts pages with an "AI model" tag. It auto-resolves a micro-leak alert when the
    alarm stays quiet.
  - Valve: by default the model only ALERTS. Setting MODEL_AUTO_SHUTOFF=true lets it also close the valve
    when the burst alarm is active, its probability is at least 0.7, and the pump is still running (latest
    reading at least 2 L/min and under 2 minutes old). Such an alert is never auto-resolved: a person must
    mark it fixed before supply can be restored (this reuses the existing safety lock).
  - The rule-based detector still runs: a flow of 30 L/min or more for 3 consecutive readings closes the
    valve (3 readings, so one 769 L/min sensor glitch cannot cut off a household). It cannot fire on the
    loop (max 4 L/min).


5. THE USAGE FORECAST MODEL
---------------------------
5.1 The task
  At the end of each day, predict how many litres the household will use on each of the next 7 days, with
  an 80% range. Use only that household's own recent daily totals (no house type or number of people -
  a real device does not know them).

5.2 Inputs (features)
  - The last 14 daily totals, each divided by the median of those 14 days (so one model suits a 1-person
    and a 5-person home).
  - Mean and standard deviation of the last 7 days, the mean of 14, the minimum of the last 3.
  - The value 7 days before the target day.
  - The target day's weekday (as sine/cosine) and a weekend flag; the horizon (1 to 7 days ahead).
  - Optional calendar inputs for the target day: maximum temperature, rainfall, a "rainy" flag (5 mm or
    more), public-holiday flag, day-before/after-holiday flag.

5.3 The algorithm: gradient boosting regression
  scikit-learn HistGradientBoostingRegressor (300 iterations, learning rate 0.05, 15 leaves). One model
  covers all 7 horizons. It predicts the ratio to the household's median and is multiplied back.
  Why: tabular lag features, small data, fast, no heavy infrastructure. I did not use an LSTM or Prophet
  because there is little data (30 simulated households x 10 weeks), nothing to gain from deep sequence
  models, and a simple model is easier to explain and to retrain.

5.4 The prediction range
  The 80% range is the forecast plus or minus the 80th percentile of the absolute error at that horizon,
  measured on the OTHER folds. Measured coverage is 80%, as intended.

5.5 Testing
  6-fold cross-validation by household. The test rows are each held-out household's last 21 days of
  forecast starting points. Baselines: "same weekday last week" and "mean of the last 7 days".

5.6 Results (mean absolute error in litres per day; lower is better)
  Pattern-free simulated data (SIMDEUM as generated):
        same weekday last week   48.5
        mean of last 7 days      37.1
        gradient boosting        37.5      -> no better than the average
  With assumed weekly / absence / guest patterns:
        same weekday last week   60.7
        mean of last 7 days      49.4
        gradient boosting        45.6      -> about 8% better than the average
  With assumed weather and holiday effects, without and with the calendar inputs:
        mean of last 7 days      51.2
        gradient boosting        47.6
        gradient boosting + weather and holidays   46.9    -> about 1.3% better
  Error on 7-day totals (what the run-out date and bill use): on the patterned data 188 L against 221 L
  for the average (about 15% better, error about 31% of the true total); on the weather data 190 L
  against 226 L.
  Importance of the calendar inputs (weather dataset): maximum temperature, rainfall and the holiday flag
  all rank among the top 10 features.
  HONEST READING: a single day is inherently noisy (about 20 to 40% error), and the gains above come from
  patterns I put into the data. The true value of the weather and holiday inputs can only be measured
  once real usage history exists.

5.7 Weather and holidays
  - Weather: Open-Meteo (free, no key). History comes from a reanalysis on a 10 to 25 km grid - good for
    temperature, only roughly right for rainfall. The forecast is reasonable for 2 to 3 days and rough
    beyond that. Cached on disk and refreshed every 3 hours in the background; if the internet is down the
    last values are used, and if there are none the forecast silently uses the model without weather.
  - Holidays: the Python "holidays" package for Sri Lanka (Poya days, New Year, Vesak, etc.) plus a file
    (holidays_override.csv) for gazette corrections, since moon-dependent dates such as Eid can be a day
    out.
  - Location is one default (Colombo); devices do not store coordinates.

5.8 What the backend does with the forecast
  - History: the last 14 complete local days. A day with no readings counts as UNKNOWN, not zero.
  - With 7 or more of the 14 days present the model is used (missing days are filled with the
    household's median). With 3 to 6 days it falls back to a plain average and says so. With fewer than 3
    it gives no forecast.
  - Prepaid RUN-OUT DATE: take the remaining balance and subtract the forecast day by day (today's
    remainder first, then each following day, then the average rate beyond 7 days). Gives a best estimate
    plus earliest (heavy use) and latest (light use) dates, and is capped by the plan's expiry date.
  - Postpaid BILL ESTIMATE: litres used so far in the open cycle plus the forecast to the end of an
    assumed 30-day cycle (cycles are closed by an admin, so this is an assumption), priced with the
    active tariff (rising-block tiers + fixed charge). Low / mid / high estimates.
  - DAILY GOAL: a household sets a litre target. The page shows used-so-far, expected total by midnight
    (used so far plus the forecast for the rest of today), a status (on track / close / over), the 7-day
    forecast against the goal, how many days were under goal, the effect on the balance or the bill, and
    up to 3 rule-based tips (overnight use, peak-hour share, weekend vs weekday, days forecast over goal).


6. THE REST OF THE SYSTEM (BRIEFLY)
-----------------------------------
  - Billing modes: prepaid (a purchased plan is drawn down by each reading; low balance throttles, empty
    closes the valve) and postpaid (usage accrues to a monthly bill).
  - Valves: a provider gate (admin) and a household tap; water flows only if both allow it. An unresolved
    leak alert blocks reopening the supply until someone marks it fixed.
  - Live chart: a socket pushes each new reading; the chart can also show 1 hour, 6 hours, 24 hours or 7
    days as averaged bins.
  - Roles: admin and household user; a user sees only their own devices.
  - Readings are stored in a MongoDB time-series collection and DELETED AUTOMATICALLY after 90 days.

8. LIMITATIONS - SAY THESE BEFORE THE EXAMINER DOES
---------------------------------------------------
  1. All accuracy figures are on SIMULATED data. The leaks are injected by the same family of generator
     that made the training data, so the results show the model learned what the simulator produces. They
     do not prove it finds real faults.
  2. A test on real loop readings was attempted and was INVALID: the real data covers only about 1% of the
     time, so most injected leaks fell in gaps with no readings. One continuous real run of a few hours
     would fix this and is the main piece of future work.
  3. Loop scale only. The models were trained on flows up to about 4 L/min. A real house has flows of 8 to
     15 L/min and real pipe breaks above 30 L/min. They would need retraining at that scale.
  4. SIMDEUM's statistics are Dutch defaults, scaled down to the loop - not Sri Lankan households. The
     daily litre totals (about 245 L/day for a 4-person household on the loop) follow that scaled routine;
     the real loop's daily running time has not been measured.
  5. A burst takes about 20 minutes to be flagged, and a perfectly flat unrealistic burst scored right at
     the threshold; real pump drift and dips help.
  6. Long legitimate use (garden watering for 30 minutes or more) can look like a burst. A false burst
     would be costly if shut-off is on, which is why it is off by default.
  7. The forecast's weekly, absence, weather and holiday effects are assumptions. Real gains need real
     usage history. Day-level forecasts stay noisy; multi-day totals are more reliable.
  8. Weather data is approximate (rainfall especially) and uses a single location.
  9. Security gaps: the reading endpoint trusts the device ID with no secret key, CORS is fully open, there
     is no rate limiting, and the model service has no authentication (it runs on localhost).
  10. No automated tests or CI.
  11. Sensor glitches (readings of several hundred L/min were seen) still count towards litres and billing;
     only the valve rule is protected from them.


9. WHAT I WOULD DO NEXT
-----------------------
  - A few hours of continuous real loop running to validate both models on real data.
  - Retrain the leak model on real labelled events and the forecast on real usage history.
  - Per-device secret keys, notifications (SMS or email) and a "false alarm" button that feeds labels back.
  - Appliance recognition (shower, toilet, tap) from the flow signature.
  - Tests and CI, and Docker for one-command setup.


10. KEY DESIGN DECISIONS AND WHY
--------------------------------
  - Event-level metrics and a false-alarm budget instead of accuracy, because normal minutes are 99% of
    the data and what matters to a household is "did it catch the leak, how fast, how often does it cry
    wolf".
  - Cross-validation by household, not by time, to show generalisation to new homes.
  - A simulator calibrated on the physical loop, because real labelled leaks are rare, dangerous to create
    and impossible to collect in a project timeframe.
  - Alert-only by default, because an automatic valve shut-off on an unvalidated model can cut off water
    wrongly.
  - Keeping the old rules as the baseline, and as the shut-off for huge flows.
  - A plain 7-day average as the forecast baseline, so the model's value is measured honestly.


11. GLOSSARY
------------
  Micro-leak        a continuous low flow (a dripping tap or leaking cistern) under normal use.
  Burst             a pipe break; on the loop, the pump running flat out for a long time.
  Recall            the share of real events the detector catches.
  False alarm       an alert when nothing was wrong.
  Delay             time from the event starting to the first alarm.
  Cross-validation  splitting the data into groups and testing each group on a model trained on the rest.
  Persistence       how many minutes the probability must stay high before an alarm fires.
  Ablation          removing a feature group to see whether it mattered.
  MAE / MAPE        mean absolute error in litres / as a percentage of the true value.
  Prepaid / postpaid  buying a litre plan in advance / being billed monthly for use.
  SIMDEUM           a published stochastic model of household water use.
  Time-series collection  a MongoDB collection type optimised for timestamped readings.


12. LIKELY VIVA QUESTIONS (SHORT ANSWERS)
-----------------------------------------
Q: What does your leak model actually do?
A: Each minute it reads the last few hours of flow, builds 27 features about how continuous and how large
   the flow is, and a Random Forest gives the probability of normal / micro-leak / burst. If a probability
   stays above a tuned threshold for a tuned number of minutes, an alert is raised.

Q: Why a Random Forest and not deep learning?
A: The input is a small set of engineered features, the data is limited, I needed fast per-minute
   predictions for many devices, and a forest is easy to explain and inspect. A neural network would add
   complexity without evidence of benefit at this data size.

Q: How is it trained? What data?
A: On 30 simulated households (SIMDEUM routines scaled to the loop and passed through a sensor model
   calibrated on the real device) with 180 injected leaks and bursts. Tested by 6-fold cross-validation
   across households, so each test household was never seen in training.

Q: Why not simply use accuracy?
A: 99% of minutes are normal, so a model that never alarms scores 99%. I report how many leaks are caught,
   how quickly, and how many false alarms per day.

Q: What are your results?
A: Micro-leaks 99% caught in about 10 minutes, bursts 92% in about 19 minutes, 0.08 false alarms per day.
   On 8 extra unseen datasets, 24/24 and 16/16 with 0.01 false alarms per day.

Q: Why did the old rule catch 0%?
A: The old rule flags a burst at 30 L/min or more, and the loop's pump never exceeds about 4 L/min, so it
   cannot ever fire. It is a mismatch between a mains-scale threshold and the loop, not a claim that rules
   are always useless.

Q: Is the data real?
A: The flow behaviour, noise, timing and sensor are measured from the real loop; the household routines
   are simulated and the leaks are injected. I say this explicitly. Real validation is future work.

Q: Is training on simulated data valid?
A: It is a reasonable first step when real labelled leaks are not available, and the simulator is
   calibrated on measurements. But the results show the pipeline and the model design work, not real-world
   accuracy. I used generators from two different families as a partial check.

Q: How do you prevent data leakage?
A: Features use only past data; whole households are held out in cross-validation; alarm thresholds are
   tuned on separate validation households, never on test ones.

Q: Why does a burst need 20 minutes to be flagged?
A: On the loop, a burst looks like the pump running flat out. A long legitimate use such as watering or
   filling a tank can last up to 30 minutes, so short flows cannot be called bursts. The persistence value
   was chosen automatically to stay under the false-alarm budget.

Q: What is the Isolation Forest for?
A: It is an unsupervised comparison: trained only on normal data, it flags anything unusual. It needs no
   labelled leaks, so it is the fallback when labels are not available. It was less accurate.

Q: What happens if the model service is down?
A: The backend logs an error and the rule-based detector keeps working. The Usage page falls back to its
   simple estimate.

Q: Does the model close the valve?
A: Not by default. It can, by a setting, when the burst alarm is active, confidence is at least 70% and the
   pump is still running. Such an alert needs a person to mark it fixed.

Q: How does the forecast work and why is it not much better than an average?
A: A gradient boosting model on the last 14 days (relative to the household's median). In pure simulated
   data, daily use has no pattern and no memory, so the average is already the best possible forecast and
   the model matches it. When weekly, absence and weather patterns exist, it is 8% better.

Q: Where do the weather effects come from?
A: The weather and holiday dates are real; the size of their effect on usage in the training data is my
   assumption (for example +3% per degree). The model learning them shows the pipeline works. Real effect
   sizes need real history.

Q: How accurate is the run-out date?
A: It is as accurate as the daily forecast, but errors partly cancel over several days. A 7-day total has
   an error of about 15 to 30%, so it reports an earliest-latest range and not a single moment.

Q: Why did you choose a 30-day bill cycle?
A: Cycles are closed manually by an admin, so there is no fixed end date. 30 days is a documented
   assumption, set by one constant.

Q: What are the privacy implications?
A: Fine-grained usage reveals when people are home. Data is tied to accounts, visible only to the owner
   and admins, and should be encrypted and access-controlled in a real deployment.

Q: What would make it work on a real house?
A: Collect real labelled readings, retrain at household flow scale, measure real false-alarm rates, add
   device authentication, and keep the shut-off as a person-approved action until validated.

Q: What did you contribute versus use from libraries?
A: The calibrated simulator pipeline, the feature design, the alarm tuning, the evaluation method, the
   service and backend integration, the forecast with run-out/bill/goal features, and the UI. I used
   scikit-learn, pySIMDEUM, FastAPI, Open-Meteo and the holidays package as tools.
